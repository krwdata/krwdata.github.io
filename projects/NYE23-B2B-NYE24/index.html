01

Everything I've played, by key

The Camelot wheel simplifies the circle of fifths so that keys which sound good together end up next to each other: same number is a relative major/minor pair, one step around is a fifth away. Outer ring is major, inner ring is minor, and each number keeps the hue a DJ is familiar with.

Every dot is one track. Each key gets the same faintly tinted slot and its tracks are scattered inside it, so the keys I live in read as dense clusters and the ones I have barely touched still read as something.

My library leans minor and clusters hard in a few keys — which is less about taste than about what disco, funk and house are mostly written in.

One night, by tempo

Each dot is a track, placed by the time it started and the BPM Serato analysed it at. Four and a half hours, from a slow open to whatever was happening at two in the morning.

03

The shape of the room

Track-to-track BPM is noisy, so this is a rolling mean across nine tracks. It opens in the 80s, is up past 120 within half an hour, and then holds a band roughly between 115 and 126 for three hours straight.

The deep notches are real and they are not mistakes. Each is a slow record dropped deliberately into the middle of a fast stretch — the thing you do when a room needs a breath, or a singalong.

04

Midnight

The Beach Boys' Auld Lang Syne starts at 23:57:41 and runs through the countdown; the first record of the new year lands at 00:00:01. Three minutes, one song, and the room turning over.

This is the argument for keeping data at the event level rather than the summary. The average BPM of this night is 114, which tells you nothing about it. The timestamps give you the countdown, the slow open and every notch in between.

⇄ The A/B test

Arm A: last year's me. Arm B: this year's.

The history contains exactly two New Year's Eve parties, twelve months apart. Same night of the year, same job, same kind of room — and a year of practice as the only thing I deliberately changed. That is close enough to an A/B test to be worth borrowing the structure from one: two arms, a fixed set of measures, and a readout. It is also far enough from one that the very next thing on this page is a precise account of the difference — because if I am going to use the words, I should be the one to say where they stop applying.

This is not an A/B test — and that matters, so here is the difference

I have borrowed the structure because it is the right way to lay this out, but an A/B test is a specific thing and this fails four of its requirements: assignment was the calendar, not randomisation; the arms are twelve months apart rather than concurrent, so everything that happened in between is confounded with the result; n = 1 per arm; and the treatment was never administered — "a year of practice" is a story about the gap, not an intervention I applied. The metrics were chosen after I saw the data, and a crowd, a room and a controller all changed too. No p-value appears on this page deliberately: the thing that changed applies to the night, so counting — and — transitions as replicates would be pseudoreplication dressed as significance. What survives is narrower and worth more: these are measurements, not estimates — the median jump really did go from — to — — and whatever explains the tempo change still has to explain why harmonic mixing did not budge, which is why I read these two skills as separable.

The result

Tempo discipline moved a long way. Harmonic mixing did not move at all. The median jump between consecutive tracks went from — BPM to —, and transitions landing inside two BPM went from — to —. Over the same twelve months, transitions leaving the Camelot wheel went from — to — — up, not down — and the longest unbroken harmonic run was — on both nights. Identical.

That row is grey in the readout rather than red, and the distinction matters: leaving the wheel is a choice, not a defect, so there is no direction it ought to move. What is damning is not the sign. It is that a year of deliberate practice moved the two tempo rows by 19 and 15 points and left this one sitting still.

I would not have predicted the split. If asked, I would have guessed both numbers drifted the same way, because "getting better at DJing" felt like one skill rather than several. It is at least two, and I was only practising one of them.

One equipment confound is worth naming, because it points the same way as the finding. Arm B was played on a —, whose tempo slider is shallow: a short physical throw, and reaching a wide enough pitch range for a big adjustment takes extra steps first. Small moves are fiddly on a fader that short. The practical effect is that large tempo changes are more trouble on arm B's controller than on arm A's — so some of the shift toward closer tempos may be the hardware discouraging the big jumps rather than me choosing against them. It cannot explain the harmonic result, which needs no fader at all, and that asymmetry is exactly why the separable-skills claim survives it. But it does mean the tempo number is the one I would trust least of the six until a third night is on a controller I can hold constant.

62% of my transitions go somewhere the Camelot wheel would not send me. The remaining 38% are the classic compatible moves: a neighbour on the wheel (16%), the same key again (14%), a two-step energy lift (6%), or the relative major or minor (2%).

If I had been asked before running any of this, I would have guessed the opposite ratio. That gap between what I believed and what I did is the only genuinely useful output of the whole project — and it is the reason the A/B test above is worth more than either night on its own.

→ The verdict

What three years of my own data says

  • Tempo is the constraint I actually respect, and it is the one that improved. Median tempo change between tracks across everything: 3 BPM. Between the two New Year's Eves it went — → —, and transitions inside two BPM went — → —.
  • Key is not, and it did not. 62% of transitions leave the wheel entirely, consistently, across three years and — sets. Between the two comparable nights it moved the wrong way, — → —. This is not drift or a bad night; it is how I play.
  • Skill is not one number. That is the finding I did not have before drawing the two nights together. One thing got measurably better while the other sat still, and I had been carrying both around as a single sense of "getting better".
  • The sets are long. — hours over — sets, averaging over an hour and a half each, with a marked preference for a slow open and a genuinely slow close.
  • Nine tracks in ten are streamed. Which is a reasonable argument for tagging a core crate locally if I ever want one.

The obvious next move is to stop describing and start predicting: given the track playing now, what did I reach for next? There is enough data here for a simple transition model, and the interesting question is not whether it can predict me — it is which of its mistakes are better than what I actually did.

The less obvious one is to fix the thing wrong with the test above: n = 1 per arm. I now have a baseline and a paired design that works, and New Year's Eve happens once a year whether I measure it or not. Reading the same six numbers off it every year costs nothing and turns one pair into a series — at which point "did I get better" stops being a story and starts being a trend line. That is the same argument I would make about any metric worth watching, which is why it belongs on this page and not just in my head.

I would have told you I mixed in key. Three years of timestamps disagree — and the year I spent getting better did not touch it. The point of measuring anything
