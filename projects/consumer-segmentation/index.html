Five million rows of telemetry, and six ways people actually cook

Marketing and product both wanted to talk about "our customers" with something behind the phrase. The company had years of connected-grill telemetry and no behavioural view of it at all — every row described a cook, and nothing described a cooker.

Input
5M+ rows, 90 days of cook telemetry
Pipeline
dbt on TimescaleDB → device features in R
Method
k-means, cross-checked with hierarchical clustering
Outcome
Segmentation report that shaped marketing and new-product direction
←Past ●Present →Future

← Past

Customer archetypes, held privately in everyone's head

Everyone who works on a grill has a mental model of who buys one. The competition-barbecue obsessive. The person who wanted a smoker and uses it as an oven. The weekend host. All plausible, none measured.

Meanwhile the telemetry sat there at the wrong grain to be useful. A row per sensor reading per cook is a fine thing to store and a hopeless thing to reason about. Nobody had turned it into a description of a person.

So the work was less about clustering — that part is a library call — and more about defining what a behavioural feature even is for a grill, then getting five million rows down to one row per device without losing the behaviour on the way.

A note on the numbers on this page

The pipeline, the features and the method are real. The device cloud and the segment names are generated by a seeded script that preserves the shape of the result — a dominant middle group, a long-cook tail, a small high-engagement cluster — without publishing anyone's data. Real segment labels and sizes are not reproduced.

● Present

From sensor readings to somebody's Saturday

Twelve SQL CTEs per monthly model, materialised through dbt, aggregated to device level in R, then clustered.

01

Most rows are not a cook

Filters first, and they are opinionated: drop cooks under five minutes, drop cooks with no valid starting set point, drop anything with an average temperature low enough to be a sensor glitch rather than food.

One cook in the raw data ran for seventeen days. Finding it was a good reminder that "average cook duration" is a number you have to earn.

02

One row per device

Then roll cooks up to the device that did them: average duration, average set temperature, how often the smoke mode is on, what share of cooks happen at the weekend, how many distinct set points get used in a single cook, how often someone raises the target mid-cook.

That last pair matters more than it looks. They are the difference between someone operating a grill and someone managing a fire.

03

The cloud, before anyone labels it

Every dot is one device, positioned by how long it cooks and how hot. There is structure in here — the dense low-left mass, the wisps heading right and up — but nothing that resolves into groups by eye. Which is the entire argument for clustering rather than eyeballing.

04

Choosing k honestly

Run k-means for every k from two to eight and plot how much variance each one leaves behind. There is no correct answer, only the point where another split stops buying much — and then a judgement call about whether the new group is a real behaviour or a slice of an existing one.

I ran four, five and six. Four was too coarse to be actionable. Five split a genuinely distinct very-long-cook group out of the low-and-slow crowd. Six was the last split that produced a group I could describe in a sentence without hedging.

05

Six segments

And they hold up under a different method: hierarchical clustering on the same features recovers substantially the same groups. That agreement is the closest thing to validation an unsupervised model gets, and it is worth doing before anyone builds a campaign on top of it.

06

The same devices, a different projection

Swap the vertical axis to smoke-mode usage and the groups reorganise. No dot has moved in feature space — this is the same six clusters seen from another angle, which is a useful thing to show people who are suspicious that the segments are an artefact of one chart.

07

The biggest group is the least romantic

A third of devices set one temperature in the middle of the dial and leave it there. Not low and slow, not searing — roasting, at 350, once or twice a week.

That was the finding that changed a conversation. The marketing image of this product category is a competition pit master. The modal user is making dinner.

08

And the smallest group is the loudest

At the other end: people who use three or four set points in a single cook and raise the target mid-way through more than half the time. Reverse sears, multi-stage cooks, active fire management. Small in number, and almost certainly the people who post about it.

09

Plus the ones who go all night

Ten-hour-plus cooks, mostly starting Friday or Saturday evening, smoke mode on for most of it. Behaviourally a subset of the long-burn crowd rather than a separate species — which is exactly the kind of thing a dendrogram tells you and a bar chart of segment sizes does not.

What I would flag to anyone using this

  • Ninety days is a season, not a year. The warehouse tiers data to cold storage after ninety days, so the window was fixed for me. Grilling is seasonal; a summer window and a winter window will not produce the same segment sizes. The archetypes look stable, the proportions almost certainly are not.
  • Engaged devices only. Devices below a minimum cook count are excluded, which means this describes people who use the product, not people who bought it. Those are different populations and the second one is arguably more interesting to marketing.
  • Two features had to be dropped. Probe usage — wired and Bluetooth — was too sparse in this window to cluster on. That is a shame, because whether someone uses a meat probe is probably the single most diagnostic thing about how seriously they cook.
Cluster labels are a hypothesis with a nice name attached. Written at the top of the report

→ Future

A segment should be a column, not a slide

This shipped as a report. Reports go stale the day they are written. The version worth building is a model that runs on a schedule and writes a segment onto every device.

Three things follow from that, in the order I would build them:

  • Make it a scheduled dbt model. Same features, run monthly, segment assignment materialised as a column. Then anyone querying device data gets behaviour for free, and nobody has to ask me to re-run anything.
  • Watch devices move between segments. Far more interesting than the static picture: someone who cooked hot and fast for a year and starts doing ten-hour weekend cooks has changed, and that change is a better trigger for a recipe recommendation or an accessory than any demographic ever will be.
  • Join it to everything else. Segment against product line, against support contacts, against the churn model on the next page over. "Which segment stops cooking after a hardware error" is a question neither model can answer alone.

And fix the probe features, which needs a conversation with the data team rather than more SQL.
