⚠ Problem

“Do the error codes tell us anything worth engineering around?”

A product line was up for a refresh. The team planning it had a reasonable question and no way to answer it: across every connected grill in the field, do component-level errors show trends that should shape the next generation?

The telemetry existed. Every connected grill reports alerts — fan, igniter, auger, temperature sensor, over-temperature, plus a long tail of things that are not failures at all. Nobody had ever aggregated it by component and looked at it over time.

So I built a small dbt project against the IoT warehouse: a staging model that isolates the device family, and a core model that rolls alerts up by week, device type and error category. Three decisions in that model did most of the work:

  • Exact ID matching, not string matching. Each category is defined by a specific source and alert ID pair. Fuzzy matching on alert text would have quietly swept in every renamed alert in the warehouse.
  • Noise excluded by design. Pellet-level warnings, lid-open events, grease reminders, low-temperature alerts and sensors that do not exist on this platform are all out. They are user behaviour or housekeeping, not hardware.
  • Percent of cooks affected, deduplicated at cook level. One failing sensor can emit a dozen alert rows in one cook. Counting rows measures how chatty the firmware is. Counting cooks measures how often someone's dinner was affected.

That last one only became obvious after the first version. I started with raw monthly counts, and the chart was beautiful and useless: it tracked fleet growth. More grills, more errors. Normalising against total cooks and dropping to a weekly grain was the difference between a picture of sales and a picture of reliability.

A note on the numbers on this page

Every chart here is generated from a seeded synthetic model that preserves the shape of the real analysis — the fleet ramp, the seasonality, the ranking of components, the bug — and none of its values. The generator is gen_woodridge.py, and the method is worth as much as the result.

🔍 Root cause, part one: the data

The model said 1,200% of cooks had an error

Before you can find out whether a component is failing, you have to be sure the thing measuring it isn't. Mine wasn't.

01

A rate that can't exist

An error rate is cooks-with-an-error divided by cooks. It cannot exceed 100%. Mine reached four figures in some weeks, which is the most useful kind of bug: one that announces itself.

02

The worst weeks are the smallest weeks

Zooming into the launch period explains half of it. Early on, the fleet is a few hundred devices and a week might contain a few dozen cooks. Any alert that had no matching cook session — an engineering unit, a dropped record — went into the numerator with nothing beneath it in the denominator. Against thirty-six cooks, a few hundred stray alerts produce a four-figure percentage.

03

The other half: two clocks

Here are ten cooks either side of a single week boundary. Nothing is wrong with any of them. The problem is in how the two halves of my calculation decided which week each one belonged to.

04

Two timestamps, two answers

The denominator counted a cook by start_time_of_cook — the black dot. The numerator counted it by the alert's observed_at — the orange one. For most cooks those fall minutes apart and it makes no difference at all.

05

Until the cook crosses midnight on Sunday

A brisket that goes on at nine on Saturday night and throws a sensor error at seven the next morning is counted in week N by one side of the fraction and week N+1 by the other. Do that for two years and some weeks accumulate errors with no cooks to divide them by.

Five of these ten cooks straddle the boundary. In a fleet doing hundreds of thousands of cooks a week, that is not a rounding error — it is a systematic drift that never cancels out.

06

One anchor

The fix is three lines: join the alerts through the cook session, anchor both sides of the fraction to the cook's start time, and drop alerts whose cook ID has no matching session at all. Same data, same query, same fleet. Watch the axis.

07

And now there's a signal

With the noise gone, one series moves and four don't. Temperature-sensor errors run elevated for the first half of the timeline and taper through the second, after a revised component enters production. The taper is slow rather than a cliff, which is what you'd expect: units built with the earlier part keep shipping and stay in the field for years.

That result mattered for a reason beyond the chart. The sensor issue was already known and already fixed. The model recovered it independently, from telemetry alone, without being told to look — which is how you find out whether a model is worth trusting on the things you don't already know.

The fix, in full

The numerator CTE now joins alerts to the cook session and takes its week from the same column the denominator uses.

-- Use cook start time (same anchor as total_cooks) so numerator and
-- denominator are always aligned to the same week. The join also
-- naturally excludes alerts whose cook_id has no cook session.
select
    date_trunc('week', cook.start_time_of_cook)::date as error_week,
    ...
from alerts
join cook_sessions cook
    on  cook.cook_id     = alerts.cook_id
    and cook.device_uuid = alerts.device_uuid
where alerts.severity in ('WARNING', 'CRITICAL')
  and alerts.cook_id is not null

Simplified from the production model; table names generalised.

I have a root cause analysis certification and five-whys training, and the honest version of this story is that the certification didn't find the bug — the impossible number did. What the training gives you is the discipline to keep asking after the first plausible answer. "Alerts without cooks" explained the launch weeks and would have been a satisfying place to stop. It did not explain the drift in the weeks after that. The second anchor problem was underneath it.

🔍 Root cause, part two: the question

A correct chart that still couldn't answer the question

The model was right and the finding was thin. I took it to the electrical engineering team, and the conversation was the most useful part of the project.

Igniter errors are the clearest example. The igniter fires, the grill doesn't light, an error is logged. That happens when the igniter has failed — and it happens when the hopper is empty. The alert is identical. Raw error frequency counts both, so a ranking of error frequency is partly a ranking of how often people run out of pellets.

High-temperature events have the same problem in reverse: they spike in summer and during long cooks, and mostly describe what was being cooked rather than the grill's condition.

So the frequency chart was accurate and not decision-grade. What the refresh team actually needed to know was not which error is most common. It was which error ends the relationship.

Do grills that throw an igniter error stop cooking, or do they carry on? The question that reframed the project

✓ Fix

Churn attribution: measuring the errors people don't come back from

A second model, at device grain instead of week grain, asking a behavioural question of the same telemetry.

01

Start with everything

Every connected device across the four SKUs in the family. Most of the interesting work in this model is deciding which of them the analysis is allowed to speak about.

02

Active: five or more lifetime cooks

Plenty of grills are registered, used twice and forgotten. If someone never really started, their silence says nothing about the hardware. Five cooks is a low bar that removes a lot of noise.

03

Churned: eight weeks of silence

Long enough to survive a holiday, a bad month, and most of an off-season. Grilling is seasonal, so this threshold is doing real work — anything much shorter and the model would rediscover winter every year.

04

Attributed: an error in the last five cooks

The device went quiet, and a qualifying hardware error appears inside its final five sessions. This is the most conservative attribution I could write: the error has to be visible in the last stretch of use, immediately before the silence.

Igniter errors that fire on the same cook as a pellet-level warning are excluded outright. That grill ran out of fuel; it didn't break.

05

Now rank the components

This is the chart the refresh team could use. It is a different ranking from raw error frequency, because it weights each error by whether the owner ever came back.

06

Two components, two thirds of it

The temperature sensor and the igniter dominate. Over-temperature events are a distant third. Fan and auger errors are loud in the logs and almost never precede a grill going quiet — which is exactly the kind of thing you want to know before spending a bill of materials increase on them.

What this number is not

Error-attributed churn is a floor, not a ceiling, and the report said so in the first paragraph. The model can only see failures the grill logged. It cannot see the intermittent fault that never threw an alert, or the owner who gave up after the third annoying cook and long before anything registered as an error. Every device in that category is invisible here and is counted as ordinary churn.

There is a second caveat worth stating plainly: some devices in the fleet are our own test units. We don't maintain a separate database for them, so a fraction of the churn signal is engineers doing engineering things. It doesn't move the ranking, but it means the absolute counts should be read as an order of magnitude.

I'd rather deliver a floor with its limits written down than a point estimate that quietly overclaims. The team using it is making a bill-of-materials decision; they need to know which way the number can be wrong.

What shipped

Weekly error-rate and churn dashboards, in Grafana for people who live there and as a standalone interactive HTML build for people who don't — that second version existed purely because dashboard access was a bottleneck, and it took an afternoon in R to remove the bottleneck entirely.

The follow-up I'd want next is customer-experience data joined onto this: how many replacement kits get shipped per component, and what the support calls say. Telemetry can tell you a grill went quiet after a sensor error. It can't tell you the owner called twice first.
