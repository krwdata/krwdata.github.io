⌘ The data

Serato will export a CSV. The CSV is a lie of omission.

The History panel exports one session at a time and quietly drops rows — short plays, second loads of the same track onto the other deck. Everything I wanted was in the binary files underneath.

The .session files in Serato's History folder use an undocumented but very simple container: a four-byte tag, a four-byte big-endian length, then that many bytes of payload, repeated to the end of the file. Nested one level down, the same structure repeats — except the "tag" is a numeric field id instead of ASCII. Strings are UTF-16 big-endian.

Which leaves the actual problem: nothing tells you what field 15 means. I dumped every field across all 563 session files, then cross-referenced against the four sessions where a hand-exported CSV also existed. Field 15 lines up with the BPM column, 51 with key, 31 with deck, 45 with play duration. That is the whole method — it is the same thing as validating a model against a known-good sample, just with fewer libraries.

One field never leaves the parser. Field 2 is the absolute path of the audio file, which on my machine means my home directory. It gets read to work out whether a track was a local file or a stream, and then discarded. The raw library is not in this repo; only the derived JSON is.

What the coverage looked like

— of tracks carry a musical key and effectively all of them carry a BPM, which is enough for everything below. Genre does not: — of what I play is streamed, and streamed tracks arrive without a genre tag, so only about one play in twenty has one. The genre streamgraph I had sketched out died there. Reporting what the data cannot support is part of the job.

● One night

New Year's Eve, —

— tracks, — minutes, 21:32 to 01:47. I did not pick this set because of the date. I picked it because it was the longest well-keyed set in the history — and then noticed what was sitting in it at 23:59.

01

Everything I've played, by key

The Camelot wheel renumbers the circle of fifths so that keys which sound good together end up next to each other: same number is a relative major/minor pair, one step around is a fifth away. Outer ring is major, inner ring is minor.

My library leans minor and clusters hard in a few keys — which is less about taste than about what disco, funk and house are mostly written in.

02

One night, by tempo

Each dot is a track, placed by the time it started and the BPM Serato analysed it at. Four hours, from a slow open to whatever was happening at one in the morning.

03

The shape of the room

Track-to-track BPM is noisy, so this is a rolling mean across nine tracks. It opens in the 80s, is up past 120 within half an hour, and then holds a band roughly between 115 and 126 for three hours straight.

The two deep notches are real and they are not mistakes. Both are slow records dropped deliberately into the middle of a fast stretch — the thing you do when a room needs a breath, or a singalong.

04

23:59

I did not tag this. Nothing in the pipeline knows what New Year's Eve is. The Beach Boys' Auld Lang Syne starts at 23:59 and runs into midnight, because that is what happened in the room.

It is a small thing and it is also the whole argument for working with event-level data rather than summaries. The average BPM of this night tells you nothing. The timestamps tell you there was a countdown.

05

And the way out

Ninety minutes later the tempo comes off hard — the last stretch averages under 100 — and it closes on Graduation (Friends Forever) at 80 BPM, which is either a very deliberate joke or exactly the right call at 1:47am. The data does not distinguish between the two.

06

Now the harmonic version

Same night, drawn on the wheel: one line per transition, from the key I was leaving to the key I was going to. Short lines are harmonically adjacent moves. Long ones across the middle are jumps.

There are a lot of long lines.

07

The moves that stayed inside the wheel

Filtering to neighbour-on-the-wheel transitions leaves a much sparser picture, and a real one: my longest unbroken run of harmonically compatible mixes that night was three.

08

It isn't just that night

Across every set in three years — — transitions — 62% go somewhere the Camelot wheel would not send me. Roughly 30% are the classic compatible moves: same key, a neighbour, or the relative major or minor.

If I had been asked before running this, I would have guessed the opposite ratio. That gap between what I believed and what I did is the only genuinely useful output of the whole project.

09

The honest read

I am not a harmonic mixer. I mix on tempo and on era: — of my transitions stay within six BPM, the median jump is three, and the sets hold a tight tempo band for hours while the keys go wherever the next record wants to go.

That is a defensible way to play a party — a floor responds to energy and recognition long before it notices a key clash. But "I mix harmonically" was a thing I would have said about myself, and it turns out to have been a story rather than a finding.

→ The verdict

What three years of my own data says

  • Tempo is the constraint I actually respect. Median tempo change between tracks: 3 BPM. Nearly two thirds of transitions are inside a six-BPM band.
  • Key is not. 62% of transitions leave the wheel entirely, consistently, across three years and — sets. This is not drift or a bad night.
  • The sets are long. — hours over — sets, averaging over an hour and a half each, with a marked preference for a slow open and a genuinely slow close.
  • Nine tracks in ten are streamed. Which is why there is no genre analysis on this page, and a reasonable argument for tagging a core crate locally if I ever want one.

The obvious next move is to stop describing and start predicting: given the track playing now, what did I reach for next? There is enough data here for a simple transition model, and the interesting question is not whether it can predict me — it is which of its mistakes are better than what I actually did.

The less obvious one is a control experiment on myself. Now that I know the number, I could try playing more harmonically on purpose for a month and see whether the sets get better or just more correct. I suspect more correct. The data would not be able to tell me, which is a limitation worth saying out loud.

I would have told you I mixed in key. Three years of timestamps disagree. The point of measuring anything
